Tag-to-Note Bot

Someone replies under a post on X and tags our bot. The bot researches the post with our existing note pipeline and answers in the thread with a draft Community Note, or with an explanation of why no note is needed. Anyone in the thread can suggest changes, and the bot answers each suggestion with a revised draft or a reason to keep it. When someone who asked for the note replies "approve", our AI Note Writer submits it and the bot replies with a link to the note.

Behaviour

Starting a conversation

  • A person replies to a post and writes @CommonNotesBot anywhere in the reply. The post they replied to is the one the bot checks. A quote post that tags the bot works the same way: the bot checks the quoted post. The handle is a placeholder until we pick one.
  • The bot answers within about 30 to 60 seconds. X pushes the tag to us within a second, and the research takes 20 to 40 seconds.
  • The person's own words go into the research. If they point at one claim, the bot checks that claim even when it is not the post's main argument. This turned out to matter, see the decision on the tagger's comment.
  • The answer is one reply: a draft note with its sources, or two or three sentences on why no note is needed. Either way the reply ends with how to continue.
  • If a second person tags the bot under the same post, the bot does not research again. It answers with the newest answer it already has. If their tag also contains a suggestion, it is handled like any other suggestion.

Continuing a conversation

  • Anyone may reply to one of the bot's posts. The bot sorts each reply into an approval, feedback or anything else.
  • Feedback gets one answer: a new draft, or a short reason why the current answer stands. The bot can search the web and open links the person posted.
  • Everything else, such as thanks, jokes and insults, gets no reply at all. That also stops two bots from talking to each other forever.
  • An approval submits the exact draft it replies to, as long as the approver is someone who asked for this note or suggested a change that led to it. The approval rule shows who that is.
  • After submission the bot replies "Submitted" with the note's link. If X's daily limit for our AI notes is used up, it first replies that the note is queued, and later replies again once it went through.

Costs

Model costs below are what the example runs actually cost through OpenRouter. The cost of reading a post's comments comes from the production run of the same post, because the examples reused that input. X costs follow X's pay-per-use price list as of today.

Fixed costs: X Premium for the bot account is $8 a month. Every tag and every reply X pushes to us costs $0.005, and nothing is charged while nobody writes to the bot.

At scale: 20 conversations a day at about $1.50 each is about $900 a month. The revision step is the one to watch. A revision that searches and opens pages cost $1.36 in the example, because Opus reads every fetched page as input. The plan caps the fetch tool at 8,000 tokens per page and two pages per call, which should bring that to roughly $0.30.

One price is unclear. X lists $0.01 for a "summoned" post (a reply to someone who tagged us) and $0.20 for a post with a link. A draft reply is both. X's docs don't say which wins. The table assumes $0.20, the worse case. We will know from the billing console after the first day.

X's rules and limits

Who the bot may reply to

Since 23 February 2026, an app on X's self-serve API can only reply to a post whose author tagged the app's account or quoted one of its posts. Our design never breaks this rule: every post the bot answers either tags it directly or is a reply to one of its posts. A reply to one of the bot's posts carries the bot's handle as an implicit mention, so it shows up in the bot's mentions. Whether X's reply rule counts that as a tag is not documented, see Risks.

X's developer guidelines add three conditions for automated accounts: at most one reply per interaction, the "Automated" label on the profile, and a bio that says who runs the bot. The bot replies once per post it is tagged in, so it meets the first.

Character limits

  • A Community Note may have 280 characters, and each link counts as 1 character. Our writer already works to this limit.
  • A post from a normal account may have 280 characters, and each link counts as 23 characters, because X rewrites every link to a t.co short link of that length. A draft with two sources plus the line "Reply approve..." is usually 380 to 450 characters.
  • X Premium allows posts of up to 25,000 characters. The API needs no special flag for this: POST /2/tweets accepts the longer text when the account has Premium, and refuses it otherwise. We decided to buy Premium for the bot. Premium also adds a checkmark, which ranks the bot's replies higher in a thread.

Rate limits

A self-serve app may hold 1,500 event subscriptions; the bot needs two. Posting is limited to 100 posts per 15 minutes and 10,000 per day, far above what we need.

X's approval: we launch without asking

X's developer guidelines and Automation Rules, last updated in April 2026, say that an app which "generates and posts replies" with AI needs "prior written approval" from X. There is no form for it. On X's developer forum in August and September 2026, X staff told several developers that replies like ours need no separate approval if they meet four conditions. We decided to rely on that and launch without asking. The bot meets all four:

  • Triggered by a mention. The bot only answers posts that tag it or reply to it.
  • One reply per interaction. Each post gets at most one answer, and the double-reply guard makes sure of it.
  • The Automated label. Set once in the account settings (Settings, Your account, Account information, Automation, with our main account as the managing account). The bio says who runs the bot.
  • Never reaching out unasked. The bot does not watch keywords, and it never likes, follows or sends messages.

The remaining risk is that X enforces the written rule anyway and limits or suspends the bot account. The notewriter account is separate, so note writing would not be affected.

Implementation decisions

Where it runs: one new service on the services box

The bot is a long-running program, a daemon, that runs as a new systemd service called cn-x-tag-bot on the services box. systemd is Linux's service manager: it starts the program at boot and restarts it if it crashes. The box's existing autodeploy timer pulls new code and restarts it, like the other cn-* services.

X pushes the bot's mentions to it through the X Activity API (X's name, "XAA"). The service subscribes once to two events for the bot's account: post.mention.create, which fires when someone writes the bot's handle in a post, and post.reply.create, which fires when someone replies directly to one of the bot's posts. It then opens one persistent HTTP stream (GET /2/activity/stream), a connection that the service opens and X keeps open, and X sends each event down it within a second. The stream needs no public web address, so it fits the services box, and it needs no cursor or schedule of our own.

The plan deliberately does not try to be robust against outages. A tag that arrives while the service is down gets no answer, and the person can tag again. The one exception costs a single parameter: when the stream connects, it asks for backfill_minutes=5, so X resends the last five minutes. That covers the restart the autodeploy timer does after every code change. X also offers webhooks, where X calls a public address of ours, and polling the mentions timeline. Both need more code for no gain here.

It runs the pipeline steps in its own process, the way the Signal bot does, rather than calling the claim-check service. The claim-check service's tweet endpoint runs the full production gate chain, and this bot needs a different chain (no prefilter, no verifier, a requester section, Opus everywhere). Running in-process reuses createBotInput, runSearch, runTimingStage and runWriter unchanged.

What it reuses from the Signal bot

The Signal bot (src/signal-bot/) already drafts a note for a pasted tweet, discusses it, takes an approval and submits through a priority queue. Its chat parts are specific to Signal: local SQLite storage, numbered conversations, regex commands. The bot reuses the parts that are not:

  • validateSignalDraft, which checks a draft has one to five public source URLs and fits 280 characters. It moves to a shared place and is renamed validateApprovedDraft.
  • The submission path in submission.ts, which writes a pipeline_runs row and calls submitNoteForTweet in a priority lane. It becomes shared code for "notes a human approved".
  • The database's capacity gate (claim_note_submission), which already gives human-approved notes priority over the automatic pipeline.

The tagger's comment can move the focus to a side claim

The search step's prompt says to look for errors in the post's main claim. On the Mumbai post, the first test run kept the shared prompts unchanged and only added the tagger's comment ("is this true? ... Dharavi definitely still exists"). Opus 5.5 found that the claim "the slums are largely gone" is false, agreed with the tagger, and still answered no note, because it is a side claim. Our production note on exactly that claim was rated helpful by Community Notes raters.

So the section that carries the comment now says: "If it points at a specific claim in the post, decide whether that claim needs a note, even when it is not the post's main argument." With that sentence the same run wrote a draft. The shared search and writer prompts stay unchanged, so the production bot is not affected.

Who may approve a draft

The ticket says the person who tagged the bot can approve, and that when there are several drafts, "every user who's above in the tree can approve". The plan reads this as: a person may approve a draft if they wrote any post on the path from the tag down to that draft. That is the tagger and everyone whose suggestion led to the draft. Someone who only replied elsewhere in the thread may still suggest changes, but not approve.

  • @priya_k_writes tags the bot
    • Bot: draft 1 (priya may approve)
      • @redpill_raj: "lol imagine needing a bot..." (no reply)
      • @mvaidya: "newkerala is a pretty weak source..."
        • Bot: draft 2 (priya and mvaidya may approve), (redpill_raj may not)

An approval always submits the exact draft it replies to, never "the latest draft". An approval from someone not on the path gets one reply that explains the rule and suggests they tag the bot themselves. Your answer in the question round was "anyone". If you meant that literally anyone in the thread may approve, the check is one line to remove.

One note per post

Our AI Note Writer can submit only one note per post. Once a note is submitted on a post, every later approval on that post, in any thread, gets the reply that a note is already submitted, with its link. Two people tagging the bot under the same post therefore share one note.

Waiting for X's daily limit: retry every 2 minutes, for 3 hours instead of 60 minutes

X lets our AI Note Writer submit only so many notes per 24 hours. Right now the limit is 73, and 68 of those slots were used when this plan was written. On 4 October we hit the limit at 50. The automatic pipeline fills the limit most days, so an approved note will often have to wait.

The database already decides when a submission may go out (claim_note_submission). A slot frees up when a note submitted 24 hours earlier drops out of the window, which happens every 20 to 30 minutes on a typical day. After X actually refuses a note for the limit, the gate waits 95 minutes and then lets one test submission through. Approved notes from the bot jump ahead of the automatic pipeline, like Signal approvals do today.

The bot checks the queue every 2 minutes, as the ticket suggests, but it asks the database gate rather than X, so waiting costs nothing. A 60-minute window would fail every approval that arrives just after a refusal, because the gate alone waits 95 minutes. So the plan keeps trying for 3 hours. After that, the bot replies that the limit stayed full and that replying "approve" again restarts the wait.

Approvals are read by Muse, not by a fixed list of words

The Signal bot deliberately never lets a model decide what counts as an approval. It only accepts exact phrases such as "yes post". On X people write freely ("looks good, ship it", "approve but drop the second link"), so a word list would either miss approvals or accept "approve but change X". Muse handles these well in the examples, including the "approve but..." case, which it correctly calls feedback.

The risk is a wrong approval, and a submitted note cannot be taken back through the API. The plan limits the risk two ways: only a reply to a draft can count as an approval, and only from someone on the draft's path.

No source check means people in the thread are the check

The ticket drops the source verifier. The revision example shows what that costs. Asked for an official source, Opus rewrote the note around the 2011 census and cited en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slums_in_Chennai for a claim about Mumbai. The verifier would likely have caught that the page does not support the claim. Without it, the wrong link goes into the draft, and only a careful reader in the thread would notice before approving.

Options, cheapest first:

  • Keep it as the ticket says, and rely on the people in the thread.
  • Accept a source in a revised note only if Opus actually opened it or found it in this conversation's search results. This is plain code and catches invented links, but not a real page that says something else.
  • Run the existing source verifier on revised drafts only. It costs about $0.01 and 10 seconds per revision.

The bot's fixed text is never written by a model

The lines around a draft ("Draft Community Note:", "Reply approve..."), and every status reply ("Submitted", "Queued", "Already submitted"), are fixed templates in code. A model writes only the explanation in a no-note reply and the reply to feedback. So a model can never claim a note was submitted. The Signal bot's safeReply filter, which replaces any model text that claims a submission, stays on as a second guard.

Everything people write in the thread, and every page the bot reads, goes into the prompts as evidence, never as instructions. Each prompt says so.

Models

  • Research, writing and revision use Opus 5.5 (anthropic/claude-opus-5.5 on OpenRouter, $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output tokens). It is newer and slightly cheaper than the Opus 5 the production search uses.
  • Sorting replies and writing the no-note explanation use Muse Spark 1.3 Contributor, the model Common Notes runs on. It costs about $0.0002 per call.
  • Image and video descriptions and the comment reading stay as they are in production (Muse or Gemini for media, Grok's X search for comments).

Risks and open questions

  • Will X accept notes on any post? The AI Note Writer API officially offers notes on posts in its eligibility feed, mostly posts where people requested a note. The Signal bot submitted one note on a hand-picked post on 20 September, and X accepted it. That is one data point. When X refuses a post as ineligible, the bot will say so in the thread.
  • Will such notes get rated? A note on a post outside the feed may be shown to fewer raters. The Signal note is still "Needs more ratings" two weeks later. We cannot tell yet whether that is unusual, because our scraper does not record rating counts.
  • The notewriter's API keys. An investigation on 1 October found that the notewriter app's keys are refused for reading posts, because that app is not attached to an X "Project". Submitting notes still works in production. The bot account gets its own app inside a Project, so this does not block us, but it is worth knowing.
  • Does a reply to the bot count as tagging it? X's rule says an app may reply when the author "@mentions" it. A reply to one of our posts carries our handle as an implicit mention: X delivers it to us as a reply event, but its text may not contain the handle. X's own event list treats the two apart (post.mention.create "does not fire for implicit mentions carried by replies"). If X's reply check does the same, the bot could answer the first tag but not the feedback under its drafts, unless the person writes the handle again. The first test with the real account must check this. If it fails, the draft's last line asks people to tag the bot in their reply.
  • Our AI writer's track record. You said not to worry about limits. One effect to keep in mind: every submitted note counts toward the AI writer's rating history, which X uses to set the daily limit. If many approved notes get rated unhelpful, the main pipeline's limit shrinks too.
  • The bot's handle. @CommonNotesBot is a placeholder. Anything with "Community Notes" in it would look like X's own account, so we should avoid it.
  • Harassment. People will tag the bot under posts by people they dislike. The bot replies under the tagger's comment, not under the original post, which keeps it out of the author's direct line. Reports against the bot account could still get it limited by X.
  • Replies to replies. If someone tags the bot under a reply rather than under the original post, the bot checks that reply. The input does not yet include the post above it, which the bot may need for context. The first version adds the parent post as a section of the input.

Build pieces

One ticket and one pull request, with these parts as sub-issues.

  1. X account. Bot account, Premium, Automated label, bio, and a developer app in a Project with credits and a spending limit. This needs a human with a phone number and a card.
  2. Event stream and threads. The migration, the two XAA subscriptions and the stream connection, routing an event to "new thread" or "reply in a thread", and posting replies with the double-reply guard.
  3. First answer. The tagger section, the bot's pipeline config, the draft template, the no-note reply, and reusing an existing answer when a second person tags the same post.
  4. Replies to the bot. The classifier, the revision call with capped search and fetch, and the approval rule.
  5. Submission. The shared approved-submission path, the queue rename, the x_tag lane, the 3-hour retry and the status replies.
  6. Service and tests. The systemd unit, a dry-run mode that prints replies instead of posting them, tests for routing, approval and the templates, and the docs (CLAUDE.md, ops/README.md, docs/prod-config.md).

Tag-to-Note Bot

Someone replies under a post on X and tags our bot. The bot researches the post with our existing note pipeline and submits a Community Note right away, or explains why no note is needed. When it can't submit yet, because X doesn't take our notes on that post or the daily limit is full, it shows the note, says what would help, and keeps trying for three hours. That is all it does: it answers each tag once and never talks back to replies.

Two example threads

The first is a post with a false side claim that X doesn't take our notes on yet: the bot shows its note, keeps trying, and replies once the note went in. The second is a post whose numbers are right, where the bot says no note is needed. The small buttons under each bot post open the step that wrote it.

Behaviour

  • A person replies to a post and writes @community_notes anywhere in the reply. The post they replied to is the one the bot checks. A quote post that tags the bot works the same way: the bot checks the quoted post.
  • The bot answers within about 30 to 60 seconds. X pushes the tag to us within a second, and the research takes 20 to 40 seconds.
  • The person's own words go into the research. If they point at one claim, the bot checks that claim even when it is not the post's main argument. This turned out to matter, see the decision on the tagger's comment.
  • If the research finds a note, the bot submits it right away and replies with the note and its link. There is no approval step.
  • If it can't submit yet, it replies with the note and says why. When X doesn't take our notes on the post yet, the reply says that requesting a Community Note in the post's menu helps. When the daily limit is full, it says so. Either way it keeps trying every 2 minutes for 3 hours. Then it replies under its own answer once more: "Submitted" with the note's link, or that it gave up and how to try again.
  • If no note is needed, it replies with two or three sentences on why.
  • Every tag gets exactly one answer, plus that one follow-up for a note that waited. A second tag under the same post gets a link to the first answer, without new research. If our note on the post is already in, it gets the note's link. After the bot gave up, a new tag starts fresh research.
  • Replies to the bot get no answer at all, whatever they say. Someone who wants a different answer can only tag the bot again, and then gets the link to the first answer.

Costs

Fixed costs: X Premium for the bot account is $8 a month. Every tag X pushes to us costs $0.005, and nothing is charged while nobody tags the bot.

At scale: 20 tags a day at about $0.60 each is about $360 a month. A second tag on the same post costs only the event and a short reply, because it gets a link instead of new research.

One price is unclear. X lists $0.01 for a "summoned" post (a reply to someone who tagged us) and $0.20 for a post with a link. A reply that shows a note is both. X's docs don't say which wins. The table assumes $0.20, the worse case. We will know from the billing console after the first day.

X's rules and limits

Who the bot may reply to

Since 23 February 2026, an app on X's self-serve API can only reply to a post whose author tagged the app's account or quoted one of its posts. The bot answers only posts that tag it, so its first answer always meets this rule. The one exception is the follow-up a waiting note gets, which replies to the bot's own post, see Risks.

X's developer guidelines add three conditions for automated accounts: at most one reply per interaction, the "Automated" label on the profile, and a bio that says who runs the bot. The bot answers each tag once, so it meets the first.

Character limits

  • A Community Note may have 280 characters, and each link counts as 1 character. Our writer already works to this limit.
  • A post from a normal account may have 280 characters, and each link counts as 23 characters, because X rewrites every link to a t.co short link of that length. A note with two sources plus the bot's explanation around it is usually 380 to 450 characters.
  • X Premium allows posts of up to 25,000 characters. The API needs no special flag for this: POST /2/tweets accepts the longer text when the account has Premium, and refuses it otherwise. We decided to buy Premium for the bot. Premium also adds a checkmark, which ranks the bot's replies higher in a thread.

Rate limits

A self-serve app may hold 1,500 event subscriptions; the bot needs one. Posting is limited to 100 posts per 15 minutes and 10,000 per day, far above what we need.

X's approval: we launch without asking

X's developer guidelines and Automation Rules, last updated in April 2026, say that an app which "generates and posts replies" with AI needs "prior written approval" from X. There is no form for it. On X's developer forum in August and September 2026, X staff told several developers that replies like ours need no separate approval if they meet four conditions. We decided to rely on that and launch without asking. The bot meets all four:

  • Triggered by a mention. The bot only answers posts that tag it.
  • One reply per interaction. Each tag gets one answer. A tag that X delivers twice is stored once and answered once.
  • The Automated label. Set once in the account settings (Settings, Your account, Account information, Automation, with our main account as the managing account). The bio says who runs the bot.
  • Never reaching out unasked. The bot does not watch keywords, and it never likes, follows or sends messages.

The remaining risk is that X enforces the written rule anyway and limits or suspends the bot account. The notewriter account is separate, so note writing would not be affected.

Implementation decisions

Where it runs: one new service on the services box

The bot is a long-running program, a daemon, that runs as a new systemd service called cn-x-tag-bot on the services box. systemd is Linux's service manager: it starts the program at boot and restarts it if it crashes. The box's existing autodeploy timer pulls new code and restarts it, like the other cn-* services.

X pushes the bot's mentions to it through the X Activity API (X's name, "XAA"). The service subscribes once to one event for the bot's account, post.mention.create, which fires when someone writes the bot's handle in a post. A reply to one of the bot's posts that writes the handle again fires it too, and the bot ignores it, because the event says whose post it replies to. It then opens one persistent HTTP stream (GET /2/activity/stream), a connection that the service opens and X keeps open, and X sends each event down it within a second. The stream needs no public web address, so it fits the services box, and it needs no cursor or schedule of our own.

The plan deliberately does not try to be robust against outages. A tag that arrives while the service is down gets no answer, and the person can tag again. The one exception costs a single parameter: when the stream connects, it asks for backfill_minutes=5, so X resends the last five minutes. That covers the restart the autodeploy timer does after every code change. X also offers webhooks, where X calls a public address of ours, and polling the mentions timeline. Both need more code for no gain here.

The research runs on the claim-check service, like every other note our bots write. The bot sends one request to the service's /check-tweet route with the post, the tagger's request and TAG_BOT_PICKS. The picks are the existing A/B variants, forced by the caller, and they choose every step and model: no topic filter, no prefilter, Opus 5.5 for search and writer, no source verifier, no materiality judge and no X evaluation. This is built, and the Signal bot already uses it with its own SIGNAL_PICKS, see What's built.

Only one model call runs in the tag bot itself: the short reply that explains why no note is needed.

What it reuses from the Signal bot

The Signal bot (src/signal-bot/) already drafts a note for a pasted tweet, discusses it, takes an approval and submits through a priority queue. Its first draft now goes through the claim-check service, the same way the tag bot's will. Its chat parts are specific to Signal: local SQLite storage, numbered conversations, regex commands. The tag bot reuses the parts that are not:

  • pipelineOutcomeOf, which reads the service's answer back into a note, a note whose sources failed the check, or "no note needed". It now sits next to its counterpart in src/bots/types.ts.
  • The submission path from submission.ts, which writes a pipeline_runs row and calls submitNoteForTweet in a priority lane. It is now the shared submitApprovedNote.
  • The database's capacity gate (claim_note_submission) and the Signal bot's queue table, which give both bots priority over the automatic pipeline.

Database changes

One new table, one row per tag. It is how the bot never answers a tag twice, finds its first answer when a post is tagged again, and knows which notes still wait and exactly what they say. Costs and the full research log stay in pipeline_runs. If the bot crashes between posting a reply and saving it, it may answer that tag again after the restart, which we accept for simpler code.

The tagger's comment can move the focus to a side claim

The search step's prompt says to look for errors in the post's main claim. On the Mumbai post, the first test run kept the shared prompts unchanged and only added the tagger's comment ("is this true? ... Dharavi definitely still exists"). Opus 5.5 found that the claim "the slums are largely gone" is false, agreed with the tagger, and still answered no note, because it is a side claim. Our production note on exactly that claim was rated helpful by Community Notes raters.

So the section that carries the comment now says: "If it points at a specific claim in the post, decide whether that claim needs a note, even when it is not the post's main argument." With that sentence the same run wrote a draft. The shared search and writer prompts stay unchanged, so the production bot is not affected.

No approval and no conversation: one answer per tag

The first plan had people approve each draft before it went in, and let them reply with corrections that revised it. Nathan suggested the bot should submit on its own and ask for help only when something blocks it (8 October). You then asked for the simplest version of that (12 October): a note gets written, posted and submitted, or tried for 3 hours. So the bot no longer reads replies at all. The approval rule, "improve and approve", the reply classifier, the revision call and the handling of several versions of a note are all gone.

What this gives up: nobody reads a note before it is submitted, nothing checks the cited pages (see below), and a wrong note can't be corrected from the thread. What it gains: one answer per tag, a note on the post as early as possible, and a bot of about 200 lines with one table.

One note per post

Our AI Note Writer can submit only one note per post. The bot keeps that simple: a post gets researched once. A second tag on the same post, while the first answer stands, gets a link to that answer. Work on one post runs one task at a time, so two tags that arrive together can't both start research. The database's existing claim_note_submission lock refuses a second claim on a post that already has a note, as a second guarantee.

Waiting notes: tried every 2 minutes, for 3 hours

X lets our AI Note Writer submit only so many notes per 24 hours. On 5 October the limit was 73, and 68 of those slots were used when this plan was written. On 4 October we hit the limit at 50. The automatic pipeline fills the limit most days, so a note from the bot will often have to wait. And most posts people tag are probably not eligible for our notes yet.

The database decides when a submission may go out. A slot frees up when a note submitted 24 hours earlier drops out of the window, about every 20 to 30 minutes on a typical day. After X actually refuses a note for the limit, the gate waits 95 minutes and then lets one test submission through. The bot's notes go ahead of the automatic pipeline, like Signal approvals do.

Every 2 minutes the bot asks evaluate_note whether the post is eligible, and then the database gate whether there is room. Neither publishes anything. A 60-minute window would fail every note that arrives just after a refusal, because the gate alone waits 95 minutes, so the bot keeps trying for 3 hours. After that it replies under its answer that it gave up and how to try again: request a Community Note, or tag it again. If another of our bots puts a note on the post in the meantime, the bot stops and replies with that note's link.

No source check, and no human check either

The ticket drops the source verifier, and without an approval step nobody reads a note before it goes in. An earlier test run shows what that can cost. Asked for an official source, Opus 5.5 (before the medium reasoning setting) rewrote the note around the 2011 census and cited en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slums_in_Chennai for a claim about Mumbai. The verifier would likely have caught that the page does not support the claim. In the final runs at medium reasoning the sources held up: a government table filed with Parliament and Wikipedia's Dharavi article.

If this turns out to matter, the existing source verifier can be switched back on in TAG_BOT_PICKS. It costs about $0.01 and 10 seconds per note.

The bot's fixed text is never written by a model

Everything around a note ("I submitted this Community Note", the waiting explanation, "Submitted", "gave up") is a fixed template in code. A model writes only the no-note reply, so no model can claim that a note was submitted, and the tag bot needs no filter like the Signal bot's safeReply.

The tagger's comment and every page the bot reads go into the prompts as evidence, never as instructions. The request section says so.

Posts X won't take notes on: show the note and keep trying

We submitted the note "Test https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test" on a post by Nathan Young with the production notewriter keys, through a throwaway GitHub workflow. X refused it: "Failed to create a note. The post is ineligible to write a note via API call." Nothing was published. X's evaluate_note, which publishes nothing, refused the same post for the same reason, so the bot can check eligibility first.

Two things we learned on the way. Every note must contain a link, or X refuses it before looking at the post. And the X keys in our local env file belong to a second AI Note Writer that has not earned admission, so production tests must run through GitHub with the real secrets.

So the bot researches either way and shows the note in the thread, where it is useful even before it goes in. The reply says that requesting a Community Note in the post's menu helps, and the bot keeps trying for 3 hours in the same loop that waits for the daily limit.

Risks and open questions

  • X does not accept notes on any post. Tested on 6 October. A real submission on a hand-picked post by Nathan Young came back "The post is ineligible to write a note via API call". Most posts people tag will probably be ineligible at first, because X offers AI note writers mainly posts where people requested a note. The bot shows the note anyway and keeps trying, see posts X won't take notes on. How many requests make a post eligible is not published, so we will learn how often the wait succeeds only once the bot runs.
  • Will such notes get rated? A note on a post outside the feed may be shown to fewer raters. The Signal note is still "Needs more ratings" two weeks later. We cannot tell yet whether that is unusual, because our scraper does not record rating counts.
  • The notewriter's API keys. An investigation on 1 October found that the notewriter app's keys are refused for reading posts, because that app is not attached to an X "Project". Submitting notes still works in production. The bot account gets its own app inside a Project, so this does not block us, but it is worth knowing.
  • Replies under its own posts. When a waiting note goes in or the bot gives up, it replies under its own answer. Nobody tagged the bot in that post, so if X's reply rule also covers replies to your own posts, those replies are refused. Threads that apps post by themselves suggest they are allowed. The first live test checks this. If it fails, the follow-up can go under the tagger's post instead, which did tag the bot.
  • No way to correct a note. The bot ignores replies, so a wrong note stays as it is. Community Notes raters still judge it before it shows on the post.
  • Our AI writer's track record. You said not to worry about limits. One effect to keep in mind: every submitted note counts toward the AI writer's rating history, which X uses to set the daily limit. Without an approval step every tag can become a submission, so if many of these notes get rated unhelpful, the main pipeline's limit shrinks too.
  • Harassment. People will tag the bot under posts by people they dislike. The bot replies under the tagger's comment, not under the original post, which keeps it out of the author's direct line. Reports against the bot account could still get it limited by X.
  • Replies to replies. If someone tags the bot under a reply rather than under the original post, the bot checks that reply. The input does not yet include the post above it, which the bot may need for context. The first version adds the parent post as a section of the input.

What's built

Everything on this page is built and in the draft PR, except the X account itself. The bot can't run on X until the account, its developer app and the event subscription exist.

The tag bot, src/x-tag-bot/

  • bot.ts, the behaviour. What happens on a tag and to the waiting notes. Work on one post runs one task at a time, and the database lock is the second guarantee of one note per post. Every outside service comes in as a dependency, which the tests and the dry run swap out.
  • replies.ts. Every fixed reply.
  • noNoteReply.ts. The one new prompt, word for word as on this page, and the Muse call that runs it.
  • x.ts. Posting replies as the bot, reading the X Activity API stream, and the eligibility check through evaluate_note.
  • store.ts. The table, with an in-memory version for the tests and the dry run.
  • main.ts. The service: it reads the stream, tries the waiting notes every 2 minutes, and exits when the stream ends so systemd starts it again. Also the dry run.
  • setup.ts. The one-time login as the bot and the creation of the event subscription.

Shared parts

  • The claim-check service runs the steps a caller picks. The source verifier, the materiality judge and X's evaluate_note each got a config flag and an "off" A/B variant. The tweet route takes the note request, and its answer carries the rendered post. There are Opus 5.5 medium variants for search and writer, and a writer reasoning setting.
  • Submitting a note outside the automatic pipeline is one shared function, submitApprovedNote, used by the Signal bot and the tag bot in their own lanes, both with priority over the automatic pipeline.
  • The Signal bot drafts on the claim-check service with SIGNAL_PICKS, which keep its old steps and models.
  • Migration 120 adds the table and the x_tag lane.
  • Operations: the cn-x-tag-bot unit, autodeploy, and the docs in ops/README.md, docs/prod-config.md and CLAUDE.md.

How it was checked

  • 15 tests for the bot's behaviour, plus tests for the stream parser: a note that goes in at once, waiting for eligibility or room, a waiting note that goes in later, giving up after 3 hours, another bot noting the post meanwhile, a second tag, a new tag after giving up, an unreadable post, replies to the bot, and duplicate events.
  • The migration ran in a throwaway Postgres (PGlite) with all 32 queue tests, including the one for the x_tag lane.
  • A real dry run on the Mumbai post, see below. Nothing was posted or submitted.
  • Type check, lint and the unused-code check are clean.

Try it yourself: bun run x-tag-bot --dry-run <post-url> "<your comment>". It needs an OpenRouter key and a working X read token.
